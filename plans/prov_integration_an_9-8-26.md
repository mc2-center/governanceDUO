Once metadata lives in a knowledge graph, access control at the dataset level stops being sufficient, because knowledge graphs are built to be traversed. A researcher with legitimate access to a controlled dataset, plus legitimate access to public metadata, can potentially walk edges between them and infer something about the controlled data that no single access grant authorized — a re-identification risk, a cohort characteristic, a linkage that only exists because two independently-approved things sit next to each other in the graph. PROV-O doesn't prevent this by itself. What it can do is give you the derivation lineage you need to detect and gate it.

Where PROV-O actually helps
- Every entity in the knowledge graph — controlled dataset, derived summary, public metadata record — becomes a prov:Entity.
- Any time something is computed, joined, or derived from one or more sources, that's a prov:Activity with prov:used pointing at its inputs, and the output entity gets prov:wasDerivedFrom back to those inputs.
- That gives you a queryable ancestry: for any node in the graph, you can walk backward and ask "what controlled sources contributed to this, even indirectly."


What PROV-O does not do for you
- It doesn't compute risk, only ancestry. Knowing that a public summary statistic was wasDerivedFrom a controlled cohort tells you the derivation happened — it doesn't tell you whether that summary is itself safe to expose (small-cell suppression, differential privacy, aggregation thresholds). That evaluation has to happen at the point the derived artifact is published, as its own step, not inferred from the graph after the fact.
- It doesn't decide propagation rules. Does a control label travel through every derivation, forever, or decay after aggregation, or get scoped to specific fields rather than the whole entity? That's a policy design question your team has to answer explicitly — PROV-O just gives you the edges to enforce whatever rule you pick.
- It doesn't catch composite risk from independent grants. A user with valid, separate access to Dataset A and Dataset B may be able to infer something from joining them that neither AR alone permits. PROV-O can record that the join activity happened (a prov:Activity with two prov:used inputs), but it has no concept of "this specific combination crosses a re-identification threshold" — that has to be flagged as its own review category, likely by pattern (any activity with two-or-more controlled inputs from different ARs gets routed for review) rather than inferred automatically.
- It doesn't enforce anything at query time. Whatever gating you decide on has to live in a query-rewriting or filtering layer (something that consumes the PROV-O lineage and blocks or redacts based on the requesting agent's current authorizations) — SPARQL doesn't know about your ARs on its own, and neither does PROV-O.
- Revocation is a real unresolved question, not a technical one. If an AR is revoked, does everything downstream that was derived while it was active retroactively lose its labeling too — and if a public artifact already incorporated that information, has the leak already happened regardless of what the graph says now? PROV-O will faithfully show you the chain; it won't tell you what "correct" behavior is when the source authorization disappears after the derivative already exists.


The practical shape this takes, if you build it
You'd want a control label computed once, at the point a new entity is created — the max sensitivity across its full derivation ancestry — stored as an attribute on the node, not recomputed live on every query. Live ancestry traversal at query time, across a graph of any real size, gets expensive fast; this becomes closer to static taint-labeling at ingestion than a runtime provenance query. Then the enforcement layer filters what a given agent can see or traverse based on that precomputed label plus their current ARs.
The honest caveat to lead with when you present this: PROV-O is a good fit for the lineage-recording half of this problem, and it's the right ontology if you're already committed to RDF for the metadata layer. But the part that actually stops a leak — propagation policy, aggregation-safety checks, composite-join risk detection, revocation semantics — is a separate system you'd still have to design and build. If this goes in front of a technical room, say plainly that PROV-O is the record-keeping layer, not the control itself, or someone will reasonably assume adopting the ontology solves the leakage problem on its own.

Some of this relates to an open problem - how do we actually "calculate" access to graph features based on 1) source content ACLs/ARs and 2) source content combinations? A direct graph query should be able to answer 1), but 2) requires some additional context/guidance to define allowed/disallowed combinations.

You also need to delineate the Synapse governance info vs Neptune query related info; these are distinct types of information, where the Neptune query is gated behind Synapse governance, in a sense. The text above separates these, but also notes that the risk/propagation/combinatorial evaluation is not captured. To help with this, it could be helpful to define rules and relationships that should be applied to derivatives/information combinations, which could be stored in the graph (or separately) and used to establish derivative access policies when information is accessed. For example, a query that uses two info sources would check their "derivation rules" to ensure the data types can be used together without concern and the access rule could then be stored as a node in the graph for later review/retrieval. This could be encoded on a datatype/processing level, referenced as needed within the nodes, and pulled into access information associated with derivative content.
